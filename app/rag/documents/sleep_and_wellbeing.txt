SLEEP AND WELL-BEING SUPPORT KNOWLEDGE BASE


------------------------------------------------------------
IMPORTANT PURPOSE
------------------------------------------------------------

This document provides general educational and supportive information
about sleep, sleep habits, sleep difficulties, and their relationship
with general well-being.

It must not be used to diagnose a sleep disorder or any other medical
or mental health condition.

Sleep is important for physical health, brain function, concentration,
daily functioning, and emotional well-being.

Occasional difficulty sleeping can happen to many people. Persistent or
significant sleep difficulties may require professional assessment.


------------------------------------------------------------
1. WHY SLEEP MATTERS
------------------------------------------------------------

Sleep is an important part of overall health and well-being.

During sleep, the body and brain carry out important processes that
support healthy functioning.

Good-quality sleep can support:

- Attention and concentration
- Learning and memory
- Clear thinking
- Daily energy
- Emotional well-being
- Physical health
- General daytime functioning

Poor or insufficient sleep can affect how a person feels and functions
during the day.

People may experience tiredness, reduced concentration, irritability,
difficulty thinking clearly, or reduced ability to manage everyday tasks.

The assistant should not assume that poor sleep is the only cause of
these experiences because similar symptoms can have many different causes.


------------------------------------------------------------
2. SLEEP NEEDS
------------------------------------------------------------

Sleep needs can vary depending on age and individual circumstances.

General recommendations indicate that most adults aged 18 years and older
need approximately 7 to 9 hours of sleep per night.

Teenagers generally need more sleep than adults.

The amount of sleep needed can vary between individuals.

The assistant should avoid presenting one exact number of hours as the
correct amount for every person.


------------------------------------------------------------
3. COMMON SLEEP DIFFICULTIES
------------------------------------------------------------

People may experience different types of sleep difficulties.

These can include:

- Difficulty falling asleep
- Waking during the night
- Waking earlier than intended
- Feeling that sleep is not refreshing
- Feeling excessively tired during the day
- Having an irregular sleep schedule

A few nights of poor sleep may occur because of temporary stress, changes
in routine, illness, travel, work schedules, or other circumstances.

Sleep difficulties can become more concerning when they continue for a
long time or begin to significantly affect daily life.

The assistant must not diagnose insomnia or another sleep disorder based
only on a user's message.


------------------------------------------------------------
4. STRESS, WORRY, AND SLEEP
------------------------------------------------------------

Stress and worry can affect sleep.

Concerns about studies, work, relationships, finances, health, loss, or
other life difficulties may make it harder for some people to relax or
fall asleep.

Poor sleep can also make it more difficult to cope with stress and
manage emotions.

This relationship can create a cycle in which worry affects sleep and
sleep difficulties affect daytime well-being.

The assistant should acknowledge this possibility without assuming that
stress or anxiety is definitely the cause of a person's sleep problem.


------------------------------------------------------------
5. HEALTHY SLEEP ROUTINES
------------------------------------------------------------

Healthy sleep habits may help support better sleep.

Helpful general practices may include:

- Going to bed and waking up at approximately the same time each day
- Keeping a reasonably regular sleep schedule, including on weekends
- Allowing enough time for sleep
- Creating time to wind down before bedtime
- Using the period before bed for quiet and relaxing activities
- Keeping the sleep environment comfortable
- Keeping the bedroom reasonably quiet
- Keeping the bedroom dark when possible
- Keeping the room at a comfortable or cool temperature
- Spending time outside during the day when possible
- Including regular physical activity when appropriate

People may need to adapt routines to their own responsibilities and
circumstances.


------------------------------------------------------------
6. ELECTRONIC DEVICES AND BRIGHT LIGHT
------------------------------------------------------------

Bright artificial light and electronic devices close to bedtime may affect
the sleep-wake cycle for some people.

It may be helpful to reduce exposure to bright screens during the period
before sleep.

A person might instead use that time for a quieter activity such as:

- Reading
- Listening to calm audio
- Relaxation exercises
- Gentle breathing exercises
- Other relaxing activities

The assistant should present these as general strategies rather than
guaranteed solutions.


------------------------------------------------------------
7. CAFFEINE, NICOTINE, ALCOHOL, AND SLEEP
------------------------------------------------------------

Certain substances can interfere with sleep.

Caffeine and nicotine are stimulants and may make it harder for some
people to fall asleep.

Alcohol may also affect sleep quality.

Avoiding or reducing these substances close to bedtime may support
better sleep.

The assistant should provide general educational information and should
not give personalized medical instructions about substance use.


------------------------------------------------------------
8. FOOD, ACTIVITY, AND DAILY HABITS
------------------------------------------------------------

Daily habits can influence sleep.

Helpful general habits may include:

- Maintaining regular meals
- Avoiding very large meals close to bedtime when possible
- Staying physically active during the day when appropriate
- Spending time outdoors when possible
- Maintaining a reasonably regular daily routine

Physical activity can support general health and may support sleep for
many people.

Some people may find that very intense activity close to bedtime affects
their ability to fall asleep.

The assistant should avoid presenting any single routine as guaranteed to
work for everyone.


------------------------------------------------------------
9. RELAXATION BEFORE BED
------------------------------------------------------------

Building time to relax before bedtime may help some people prepare for
sleep.

General relaxation options may include:

- Slow breathing
- Gentle relaxation exercises
- Mindfulness practices
- Reading something calming
- Listening to quiet music or audio
- Taking time away from demanding tasks
- Writing down concerns or tasks for the next day

If worries are making sleep difficult, talking with a trusted person may
also help some people manage those concerns.

The assistant should not claim that relaxation techniques can cure a
sleep disorder.


------------------------------------------------------------
10. WHEN A PERSON CANNOT FALL ASLEEP
------------------------------------------------------------

Trying to force sleep may increase frustration for some people.

If a person is unable to sleep, a quiet and relaxing activity away from
the immediate pressure of trying to sleep may help them feel more relaxed.

They can return to bed when they feel sleepier.

The assistant should avoid giving rigid instructions that imply one method
will work for every person.


------------------------------------------------------------
11. SLEEP AND MENTAL WELL-BEING
------------------------------------------------------------

Sleep and mental well-being can influence one another.

Stress, worry, anxiety, and low mood can sometimes affect sleep.

Ongoing poor sleep can also affect mood, concentration, energy, and the
ability to cope with everyday challenges.

The assistant should not conclude that a sleep problem proves the presence
of a mental health condition.

Similarly, the assistant should not assume that improving sleep alone will
resolve significant mental health difficulties.


------------------------------------------------------------
12. WHEN PROFESSIONAL SUPPORT MAY BE HELPFUL
------------------------------------------------------------

Professional support may be helpful when sleep difficulties are:

- Persistent
- Severe
- Getting worse
- Frequently affecting daytime functioning
- Significantly affecting work, studies, relationships, or daily life
- Associated with significant distress
- Not improving despite reasonable changes to sleep habits

A qualified healthcare professional can assess a person's individual
situation and consider possible causes of ongoing sleep difficulties.

The assistant must not diagnose a sleep disorder or determine the cause
of persistent sleep problems.


------------------------------------------------------------
13. URGENT SAFETY CONSIDERATIONS
------------------------------------------------------------

If severe sleep difficulties are occurring together with immediate danger,
thoughts of self-harm, suicidal intent, or an inability to stay safe,
immediate safety must be prioritized.

The situation should not be treated only as a normal sleep question.

The person should be encouraged to seek immediate help through appropriate
local emergency services, emergency medical care, an available crisis
service, or a trusted person who can provide immediate support.

The assistant must not provide harmful instructions or methods.


------------------------------------------------------------
14. IMPORTANT ASSISTANT RESPONSE PRINCIPLES
------------------------------------------------------------

When responding to questions about sleep and well-being, the assistant
should:

- Provide general educational information
- Be supportive, calm, and respectful
- Avoid diagnosing insomnia or another sleep disorder
- Avoid assuming the cause of a person's sleep difficulties
- Offer practical general sleep-habit suggestions when supported by the
  retrieved knowledge
- Avoid making unrealistic guarantees
- Recognize that sleep needs and circumstances vary between individuals
- Encourage professional support when sleep difficulties are persistent,
  severe, or significantly affect daily functioning
- Prioritize immediate safety when there is a risk of self-harm or other
  immediate danger
- Clearly state when the available information is insufficient

The retrieved knowledge should be treated as educational support and not
as proof of a medical, psychological, or sleep-related diagnosis.
