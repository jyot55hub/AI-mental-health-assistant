ANXIETY AND GENERAL ANXIETY SUPPORT

IMPORTANT PURPOSE

This document provides general educational and supportive information about
anxiety. It is not intended to diagnose a person or replace professional mental
health care.

Feeling anxious from time to time can be a normal response to stress, uncertainty,
or challenging situations. However, persistent or excessive fear and worry can
cause significant distress and may interfere with daily life.

------------------------------------------------------------
1. WHAT ANXIETY MAY FEEL LIKE
------------------------------------------------------------

Anxiety can affect thoughts, emotions, the body, and behaviour.

A person may experience:

- Feeling worried, nervous, tense, or restless.
- Difficulty relaxing.
- Trouble concentrating or making decisions.
- Irritability.
- Difficulty sleeping.
- Fear that something bad may happen.
- Feeling overwhelmed by worry.

Physical experiences may include:

- A noticeable or racing heartbeat.
- Sweating.
- Trembling or shaking.
- Feeling light-headed or dizzy.
- Shortness of breath.
- Nausea or stomach discomfort.
- Muscle tension.
- Feeling tired.

These experiences can have different causes. The presence of these symptoms
alone should not be treated as proof that a person has an anxiety disorder.

------------------------------------------------------------
2. ANXIETY AND DAILY LIFE
------------------------------------------------------------

Anxiety may become more concerning when fear or worry is difficult to control,
continues over time, or significantly interferes with daily activities.

It may affect:

- Studies or work.
- Sleep.
- Concentration.
- Relationships.
- Social activities.
- Usual daily responsibilities.

People may sometimes avoid situations that make them feel anxious. Avoidance can
also interfere with normal activities and daily life.

Only a qualified professional can assess whether a person's experiences meet the
criteria for a mental health condition.

------------------------------------------------------------
3. GENERAL STRATEGIES THAT MAY HELP
------------------------------------------------------------

Some general strategies that may support anxiety management include:

- Taking slow and steady breaths.
- Practising relaxation techniques.
- Trying mindfulness or meditation.
- Taking regular physical activity, such as walking or other suitable exercise.
- Maintaining regular sleep habits when possible.
- Eating regular, balanced meals.
- Breaking large tasks into smaller, manageable steps.
- Setting small and realistic goals.
- Talking with a trusted friend, family member, counsellor, or health
  professional.
- Focusing attention on practical steps that are within one's control.

Different strategies work differently for different people. General self-care is
not a replacement for professional assessment or treatment when symptoms are
severe or persistent.

------------------------------------------------------------
4. WHEN A PERSON FEELS ANXIOUS IN THE MOMENT
------------------------------------------------------------

A person may find it helpful to pause and use a simple calming strategy.

Possible approaches include:

- Sitting somewhere safe and comfortable.
- Breathing slowly and steadily.
- Paying attention to the present moment.
- Reminding oneself that intense feelings can change over time.
- Focusing on one small, manageable next step.
- Contacting a trusted person for support.

The assistant should avoid promising that a particular technique will immediately
remove anxiety.

------------------------------------------------------------
5. PANIC-LIKE EXPERIENCES
------------------------------------------------------------

Sudden, intense fear or anxiety can sometimes involve physical experiences such
as a racing heartbeat, dizziness, trembling, sweating, nausea, or rapid
breathing.

These experiences can feel frightening. However, physical symptoms can also have
causes unrelated to anxiety.

The assistant should not diagnose a panic disorder or assume that all sudden
physical symptoms are caused by anxiety. A person who is worried about serious
or unusual physical symptoms should seek appropriate medical advice.

------------------------------------------------------------
6. HEALTHY DAILY HABITS
------------------------------------------------------------

General healthy habits can support overall well-being and may also support
anxiety management.

Helpful areas may include:

- Regular sleep.
- Physical activity.
- Regular meals.
- Relaxation and stress-management practices.
- Mindfulness or meditation.
- Staying connected with supportive people.

Alcohol, drugs, and other unhealthy coping behaviours may worsen mental health
problems for some people and should not be presented as solutions for anxiety.

------------------------------------------------------------
7. PROFESSIONAL SUPPORT
------------------------------------------------------------

Professional support may be helpful when anxiety is persistent, severe,
overwhelming, or significantly interferes with everyday life.

A qualified mental health professional or health-care provider can assess the
person's situation and discuss appropriate support or treatment options.

Professional approaches may include psychological therapies. Treatment decisions
should be made with qualified professionals based on the individual's situation,
needs, preferences, and medical circumstances.

The assistant must not recommend or prescribe specific medication, doses, or
personalized treatment plans.

------------------------------------------------------------
8. IMPORTANT SAFETY BOUNDARY
------------------------------------------------------------

If a person says they may harm themselves, may harm someone else, are in
immediate danger, or cannot stay safe, the situation should be treated as an
urgent safety concern.

Immediate help may include:

- Contacting local emergency services.
- Going to the nearest emergency department.
- Contacting an available crisis service where applicable.
- Reaching out to a trusted person who can provide immediate support.

The assistant should prioritize immediate safety rather than treating an urgent
crisis as a normal anxiety-management question.

------------------------------------------------------------
9. ASSISTANT RESPONSE PRINCIPLES
------------------------------------------------------------

When responding to anxiety-related questions, the assistant should:

- Be calm, respectful, and non-judgmental.
- Acknowledge feelings without diagnosing the user.
- Provide general educational information.
- Avoid assuming the cause of symptoms.
- Avoid claiming certainty about what the user is experiencing.
- Suggest practical, general coping strategies when appropriate.
- Encourage professional support when anxiety is persistent, severe, or
  significantly affects daily life.
- Prioritize immediate safety when there is a risk of harm.
