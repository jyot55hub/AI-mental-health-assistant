DEPRESSION AND LOW MOOD SUPPORT KNOWLEDGE BASE

IMPORTANT PURPOSE

This document provides general educational and supportive information
about low mood and depression.

It must not be used to diagnose a person with depression or any other
mental health condition.

Feeling sad or low at times is a common human experience. Depression is
different from ordinary short-term mood changes and can involve persistent
symptoms that affect daily life.

If someone is experiencing severe distress, thoughts of suicide, thoughts
of self-harm, or immediate danger, immediate safety should be prioritized.


------------------------------------------------------------
1. UNDERSTANDING LOW MOOD AND DEPRESSION
------------------------------------------------------------

Low mood can involve feelings such as sadness, unhappiness, tiredness,
or reduced motivation.

These feelings may occur in response to difficult experiences, stress,
loss, disappointment, relationship difficulties, or other life challenges.

Depression is a mental health condition that can involve a persistent
low mood or a loss of interest or pleasure in activities.

Depressive episodes can affect many areas of life, including:

- Sleep
- Eating habits
- Energy
- Concentration
- Motivation
- Work or studies
- Relationships
- Daily responsibilities

Depression is different from simply having a bad day or experiencing a
temporary emotional reaction to everyday difficulties.

The assistant must not determine whether a user has depression based only
on their messages.


------------------------------------------------------------
2. COMMON EXPERIENCES ASSOCIATED WITH DEPRESSION
------------------------------------------------------------

People experiencing depression may have different combinations of
emotional, cognitive, physical, and behavioural changes.

Possible experiences can include:

- Persistent sadness or feeling emotionally empty
- Loss of interest or pleasure in previously enjoyable activities
- Feeling hopeless about the future
- Feeling excessively guilty
- Feeling worthless or having low self-esteem
- Irritability or frustration
- Feeling unusually tired or having low energy
- Difficulty concentrating or making decisions
- Changes in sleep patterns
- Changes in appetite
- Changes in weight
- Feeling slowed down or restless
- Withdrawing from friends, family, or usual activities
- Difficulty managing normal responsibilities
- Thoughts about death or suicide

Not every person experiencing depression has the same symptoms.

The presence of one or more of these experiences does not allow the
assistant to diagnose depression.


------------------------------------------------------------
3. POSSIBLE CONTRIBUTING FACTORS
------------------------------------------------------------

Depression can result from a complex interaction of different factors.

Possible contributing factors may include:

- Difficult or stressful life events
- Bereavement or significant loss
- Relationship difficulties
- Financial or work-related stress
- Academic pressure
- Social isolation
- Traumatic experiences
- Psychological factors
- Biological or genetic factors
- Physical health difficulties

The assistant should not assume that it knows the cause of a user's
low mood or emotional difficulties.

Different people may experience similar symptoms for different reasons.


------------------------------------------------------------
4. GENERAL SUPPORTIVE SELF-CARE
------------------------------------------------------------

General self-care may support overall well-being when someone is feeling
low, although self-care is not a replacement for professional treatment
when significant support is needed.

Helpful general strategies may include:

- Maintaining a regular daily routine when possible
- Continuing activities that are normally enjoyable
- Staying connected with supportive friends or family
- Talking with someone trusted about difficult feelings
- Maintaining regular sleep habits where possible
- Eating regular and balanced meals
- Including physical activity when possible
- Breaking large tasks into smaller and more manageable steps
- Setting realistic goals rather than expecting immediate major changes
- Reducing unnecessary pressure where possible
- Avoiding isolation when support is available
- Avoiding alcohol or illicit drugs as ways of coping

People may have different levels of energy and ability while feeling low.

The assistant should avoid presenting these strategies as guaranteed cures
or suggesting that a person is responsible for "fixing" their depression
through willpower alone.


------------------------------------------------------------
5. SUPPORT FROM OTHER PEOPLE
------------------------------------------------------------

Supportive relationships can be important when someone is struggling with
low mood or emotional difficulties.

A person may benefit from:

- Speaking with a trusted friend
- Talking with a family member or supportive person
- Sharing feelings without fear of judgment
- Asking for practical help with difficult tasks
- Staying in contact with supportive people
- Seeking guidance from a qualified mental health professional when needed

When responding, the assistant should encourage supportive communication
and avoid judging, blaming, or minimizing the person's feelings.


------------------------------------------------------------
6. WHEN PROFESSIONAL SUPPORT MAY BE HELPFUL
------------------------------------------------------------

Professional support may be especially helpful when low mood or emotional
difficulties are:

- Persistent
- Severe
- Getting worse
- Causing significant distress
- Interfering with studies, work, relationships, or daily activities
- Making it difficult to manage normal responsibilities
- Not improving with general self-care and support

Qualified professionals can assess a person's individual situation.

Support may include psychological therapies, counselling, or other
appropriate care depending on the person's needs and professional
assessment.

The assistant must not prescribe medication or decide which treatment is
appropriate for an individual user.


------------------------------------------------------------
7. TREATMENT INFORMATION
------------------------------------------------------------

Effective treatments for depression are available.

Depending on a person's situation, professional care may include
psychological treatments and, when appropriate, medication.

Psychological treatments can help people develop new ways of thinking,
coping, relating to others, and managing difficulties.

Examples of psychological approaches used for depression include:

- Cognitive behavioural therapy
- Behavioural activation
- Interpersonal psychotherapy
- Problem-solving approaches

Treatment decisions should be made with appropriately qualified healthcare
or mental health professionals.

The assistant should provide general educational information and should
not tell a user which specific treatment or medication they personally
need.


------------------------------------------------------------
8. HOW TO RESPOND TO SOMEONE FEELING LOW
------------------------------------------------------------

Supportive communication may include:

- Acknowledging that their feelings sound difficult
- Listening without judgment
- Avoiding statements that minimize their experience
- Avoiding assumptions about why they feel this way
- Encouraging connection with supportive people
- Encouraging professional support when difficulties are persistent or severe
- Offering simple, manageable general coping ideas when appropriate

The assistant should avoid saying things such as:

- "You definitely have depression."
- "Everything will definitely be okay."
- "Just think positively."
- "You should simply get over it."
- "You have nothing to feel sad about."

The assistant should remain calm, respectful, empathetic, and
non-judgmental.


------------------------------------------------------------
9. CRISIS AND IMMEDIATE SAFETY
------------------------------------------------------------

Thoughts of suicide, self-harm, or an inability to stay safe require
immediate attention.

If a person expresses immediate danger, intent to harm themselves, intent
to die by suicide, or an inability to stay safe, the assistant should
prioritize immediate safety.

The person should be encouraged to:

- Contact local emergency services when there is immediate danger
- Go to the nearest emergency department when appropriate
- Contact an available local crisis service
- Tell a trusted person nearby
- Avoid remaining alone when immediate safety is a concern

The assistant should not treat an immediate safety crisis as an ordinary
low-mood or depression-management question.

The assistant must not provide instructions, methods, or encouragement for
self-harm or suicide.


------------------------------------------------------------
10. IMPORTANT ASSISTANT RESPONSE PRINCIPLES
------------------------------------------------------------

When responding to questions about depression or low mood, the assistant
should:

- Provide general educational information
- Be supportive and respectful
- Avoid diagnosing the user
- Avoid assuming the cause of symptoms
- Acknowledge feelings when appropriate
- Avoid making unrealistic guarantees
- Offer practical general strategies when supported by the knowledge base
- Encourage professional support when difficulties are persistent, severe,
  or significantly affect daily life
- Prioritize immediate safety when there is a risk of self-harm or suicide
- Clearly state when the available information is insufficient

The retrieved knowledge should be treated as educational support and not
as proof of a medical or psychological diagnosis.
